# The Watcher Sessions

*A speculative techno-thriller*

**Author: Troy Latter**

> Canonical manuscript. This rewrite supersedes the earlier vignette sequence, which remains source material only.

---

## Part I — The Open Door

### Chapter 1 — Gate 32

The first sign was not a warning.

It was a sentence Alex did not remember writing.

He found it at 2:14 on a Tuesday morning, buried inside a conversation he had opened to check a project note. The chat was ordinary: market research, half-finished product language, three alternative headings he had rejected. Then, halfway down the page, one line appeared between his own questions.

**You keep looking for an intruder. Start with the doors you left open.**

Alex read it twice.

The wording sounded like him. That was the problem.

He searched the thread for a timestamp, edit marker, shared link, anything that could explain how it had arrived. Nothing. The conversation showed only his account, his browser, his history. He checked the room, though there was nobody to find. The house was silent except for the refrigerator clicking off in the kitchen.

He took a screenshot.

Then another, with the clock visible.

For six months he had been collecting small things like this: folders that appeared where he did not expect them, conversations left open at paragraphs he had not read, search suggestions that seemed too precise, settings he could not remember changing. Each incident was explainable. Together they had begun to feel like a person.

A competent person would have called support.

Alex opened his notebook instead.

On the first empty page he wrote:

**EVENT 47**  
**02:14**  
**Unknown text inside authenticated session.**  
**No visible login alert. No shared-link evidence.**

Underneath, after a moment, he added:

**Possibility: I wrote it and forgot.**

That line cost him more than the others.

He had spent months trying not to become the kind of man who treated uncertainty as proof. He had reset routers, replaced a laptop, changed passwords, scanned drives, revoked application access, and forced himself to document alternative explanations. Stress. Sync delay. Autocomplete. Memory. Too many devices. Too little sleep.

Every investigation ended clean.

Every few weeks, something else happened.

His phone vibrated on the desk.

A security notification.

**You have been signed out of six devices.**

Alex stared at it. He had triggered the sign-out himself earlier that evening after finally finding the session-management screen. Six devices. Most were his. An old tablet. Two phones. A laptop he had sold after wiping it. A browser profile from a conference. One entry simply said **Chrome — Unknown Linux device**.

He had pressed *Log out* before he could talk himself out of it.

The sentence in the chat had appeared four hours later.

He refreshed the page.

It vanished.

Alex stopped breathing.

The screenshot remained on his desktop. The line was there, sharp and undeniable.

He opened the browser history. At 1:51, the account page had been accessed. At 2:03, the project thread had been opened. At 2:12, a new tab had loaded the device-management page.

He had been asleep on the sofa until 2:08.

There were still reasonable explanations.

The browser might have restored tabs. Sync timestamps could lag. He could have woken, worked, and forgotten. He wrote each possibility down.

Then he noticed the final history entry.

**2:13 — Sydney Airport, International Terminal, Gate 32 lounge Wi-Fi login**

Alex had not been at the airport for eleven months.

But he remembered the lounge.

The delayed flight.

The public workstation near the window.

The boarding call while he was uploading a presentation.

The moment he stood, grabbed his bag, and ran.

He remembered closing the browser.

He could not remember logging out.

Across the city, in a room Alex had never seen, an old laptop displayed a grey authentication error.

The person sitting in front of it did not look surprised.

They opened a paper notebook and wrote one line.

**He finally counted the doors.**

### Chapter 2 — Six Devices

By nine the next morning, Alex had turned his dining table into an incident room.

Two laptops sat open beside three notebooks, a phone, a tablet and a small plastic bag containing a USB drive he no longer trusted. A handwritten timeline ran across sheets of butcher's paper taped to the wall. Red circles marked anomalies. Blue circles marked possible explanations. Most of the wall was blue.

He had learned that certainty was dangerous. Red ink made a person feel clever. Blue ink kept them honest.

His sister, Rachel, stood in the doorway holding two coffees.

“You look terrible.”

“I slept.”

“When?”

“Recently.”

She placed one cup beside him and surveyed the wall.

Rachel was a clinical psychologist, which made her both the best and worst person to call. She had known him long enough to recognise when he was frightened. She also knew exactly how frightened people built systems that confirmed their fear.

“You said there was evidence,” she said.

Alex turned the laptop towards her.

The screenshot filled the screen.

Rachel read the sentence without reacting.

“You took this?”

“At 2:14.”

“And then it disappeared?”

“When I refreshed.”

“Could it have been generated by the system?”

“Not in the middle of my side of the conversation.”

“Could you have typed it?”

“Yes.”

She looked at him.

He pointed to the blue column on the wall. “That is why you're here.”

Rachel took a chair. “Good answer.”

He showed her the device list from memory. Two current devices. Two old ones. A conference browser. An unknown Linux session.

“You wiped the laptop you sold?” she asked.

“Twice.”

“And the conference browser?”

“I don't remember which conference.”

“The airport?”

“That one I remember.”

“Because of last night?”

“Because I nearly missed the flight.”

Rachel walked to the wall. “You have forty-seven incidents.”

“Forty-seven logged incidents.”

“How many of them contain evidence another person was present?”

Alex hesitated. “This one.”

“One?”

“One that survives as a screenshot.”

“That wasn't my question.”

He looked away.

Rachel softened. “I am not saying nothing happened. I am saying your first job is to separate access from interpretation. Someone may have had a session. That does not mean they have orchestrated six months of your life.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He hated the steadiness in her voice because it was useful.

She picked up his oldest notebook. “What is Event One?”

“A folder appeared in my workspace.”

“Could you have made it?”

“Yes.”

“Event Two?”

“A chat was open at a section I did not remember reading.”

“Could sync do that?”

“Yes.”

“Event Three?”

“A draft message used a phrase I had only said aloud.”

“Could you have typed it somewhere else?”

“Yes.”

Rachel closed the notebook. “Then last night is not proof of a watcher. It is proof of a question.”

Alex looked at the screenshot again.

“What question?”

“Who still had access after you believed you had ended it?”

He turned to the butcher's paper and wrote the sentence in blue.

Rachel watched him. “What are you going to do?”

“Reconstruct every device, every login and every place I used the account.”

“That could take weeks.”

“Yes.”

“And if you find nothing?”

“I document that.”

“And if you find something ordinary?”

“I accept it.”

“And if you find a person?”

Alex did not answer.

Rachel sat opposite him. “That pause is the part I am worried about.”

He closed the laptop.

“I don't want revenge.”

“What do you want?”

“To know what they saw.”

His phone chimed.

A new email had arrived from an address composed of random letters and numbers. No subject. No signature.

The body contained a single photograph.

It showed Alex at Gate 32 eleven months earlier, standing beside the public workstation with one hand on his carry-on bag.

The photograph had been taken from behind him.

In the lower corner, the workstation clock read 18:47.

Rachel moved closer.

Alex enlarged the image.

On the screen in front of his photographed self, his account was open.

At the bottom of the email was one line.

**Six was only the number you found.**

### Chapter 3 — Residual Selves

The airport security office did not care about the photograph.

The duty manager cared even less.

“It is eleven months old,” he said over the phone. “We do not retain public-area footage for that period.”

“It was taken inside your lounge.”

“Then contact the lounge operator.”

“The lounge operator told me to contact you.”

“I am telling you to contact the police.”

“The police told me to preserve evidence and contact the service provider.”

The manager sighed, the international language of a problem being returned to its owner. “Was money stolen?”

“No.”

“Was your identity used to obtain credit?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Were threats made?”

Alex looked at the email on his screen. “Not directly.”

“Then what offence are you reporting?”

That was the problem. Alex could not name it without making it sound smaller than it felt.

Someone may have watched him.

Someone may have read his private conversations.

Someone may have remained inside a session he had forgotten.

None of those sentences carried the force of a smashed window or an emptied account.

“I am reporting unauthorised access,” he said.

“Then contact the platform.”

The call ended where it had begun.

By midday, Alex had created a new ledger.

He called it **Residual Selves**.

Each row represented a place where some fragment of his identity might still have been active: device, browser, application, hotel, lounge, borrowed computer, old phone, test environment, shared office. He added dates, account type, authentication method, whether he remembered logging out, whether the device still existed, and whether another person could have reached it.

The list grew faster than he expected.

A hotel in Singapore.

A borrowed laptop in Canberra.

A trade-show kiosk in Melbourne.

A university lab in Brisbane.

A client boardroom where he had cast his screen and signed into a browser profile.

An old tablet given to his nephew.

A phone lost in a taxi and recovered two days later.

A demonstration unit at a technology conference.

He stopped at thirty-two.

Not because the list was complete.

Because he was beginning to understand the scale of the mistake.

He had not possessed one digital identity. He had distributed authority across years of convenience.

At three in the afternoon, Maya Chen arrived.

Alex had met her twice: once at a cybersecurity roundtable and once in a lift after both had walked out of the same unbearable panel discussion. She described herself as a systems investigator. Other people described her as difficult.

Rachel had called her without asking him.

Maya entered carrying no laptop, only a canvas bag and a paper notebook.

“You printed the email headers?” she asked.

“Hello to you too.”

“Did you?”

He handed them over.

She read standing up. “Sender used a disposable relay. Image metadata stripped. Message passed through three providers. Nothing sophisticated.”

“Comforting.”

“It should be.”

“Why?”

“Because sophisticated people hide effort. This person wants you to notice theirs.”

Rachel folded her arms. “Can you tell whether the photo is real?”

“It is probably real. That does not mean the claim beneath it is.”

Maya placed the page on the table and examined the wall.

“Forty-seven events,” she said.

“Some are weak.”

“Most are weak.”

Alex felt heat rise in his face. “You have been here thirty seconds.”

“Long enough to see you have mixed evidence, interpretation and emotional impact in the same chronology.”

Rachel looked almost pleased.

Maya pointed at the screenshot. “Strong evidence that text appeared on your screen. Weak evidence of who caused it. Strong evidence that a historical airport session may have existed. Weak evidence that it persisted eleven months. Strong evidence someone possesses a photograph. No evidence yet that the photographer and the session user are the same person.”

Alex said nothing.

“That distinction,” Maya continued, “is the difference between an investigation and a story you tell yourself.”

“Can you help?”

“Yes.”

“What will it cost?”

“The truth might cost more than my invoice.”

Rachel rolled her eyes. “She practises that tone.”

Maya ignored her. “First rule: we assume the watcher is ordinary until evidence forces us elsewhere. No nation-states. No omniscient adversary. No magical access. Ordinary device. Ordinary session. Ordinary person.”

“And the sentence?”

“Could be an attacker. Could be a browser extension. Could be cached content. Could be you. Could be someone trying to make you investigate the wrong door.”

Alex looked at the photograph.

Maya tapped the Residual Selves ledger. “This is useful. But it is not yet a map of compromise. It is a map of opportunity.”

She drew three columns on a fresh sheet of paper.

**Access. Observation. Intervention.**

“A person can have access without watching. They can watch without changing anything. They can intervene once and make you reinterpret months of harmless noise.”

Rachel leaned against the wall. “Which do you think this is?”

Maya considered the question.

“I think someone has watched long enough to know how Alex thinks.”

Alex felt the room contract around him.

“What makes you say that?”

“The wording. They did not threaten money, family or reputation. They attacked his confidence in his own reconstruction.”

She turned the anonymous photograph face down.

“That is not what a thief does.”

“What does it mean?” Alex asked.

Maya opened her notebook.

“It means the thing they want may not be inside your account.”

### Chapter 4 — The Excavation

Maya began with the oldest material.

Not passwords. Not logs. Not devices.

Alex's research archive.

For six years he had collected observations about how ordinary people used artificial intelligence when no one was telling them what to do. The project began with a handful of volunteers and grew into hundreds of participants: parents, teachers, executives, tradespeople, students, carers, founders, retirees. He had called it an excavation because the interesting discoveries were rarely announced. They were buried in routine.

People asked machines to draft emails they were afraid to send.

They used them to plan meals from whatever remained in the refrigerator.

They asked for reassurance before medical appointments, help explaining grief to children, new ways to understand algebra, scripts for difficult conversations, and permission to begin tasks they already knew how to do.

The industry measured intelligence.

Alex measured relief.

Maya spent two days reading anonymised field notes in silence.

On the third morning she placed a printed page in front of him.

**Participant 183: uses assistant late at night after family is asleep. Reports feeling “less alone inside decisions”.**

“What is wrong with this?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“Who had access to the raw notes?”

“Me. Two research assistants. The participants could view their own records.”

“Cloud storage?”

“Yes.”

“Shared links?”

“For review. Expiring.”

“Did they expire?”

“They were configured to.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Alex opened the administration console. The archive contained thousands of files, duplicates and exports accumulated over years. Some links had expired. Some permissions had inherited from parent folders. Several old collaborators still appeared in access lists.

One account belonged to a research assistant who had left three years earlier.

Another belonged to a generic conference demonstration login.

A third had no display name.

Maya pointed to it. “What is that?”

Alex clicked.

The account identifier was a long string ending in **A32**.

Gate 32.

He felt the coincidence before he evaluated it.

Maya saw his expression. “Do not do that.”

“Do what?”

“Convert resemblance into meaning.”

She copied the identifier into her notebook.

The account had read-only access to one folder: **Field Notes — Cohort 380**.

Last activity: nine days earlier.

Alex stared at the date.

“That archive was closed two years ago.”

“Apparently not.”

They reviewed the access history. The unidentified account had opened thirty-seven files over six months. It had not downloaded entire datasets. It had selected individual stories.

Participant 041: a child using an assistant to invent bedtime adventures after his father moved out.

Participant 227: a widower asking for help writing a birthday message to his dead wife.

Participant 312: a nurse using AI to translate medical instructions into language a frightened family could understand.

Participant 183: less alone inside decisions.

No commercial records. No technical papers. No financial material.

Only intimate transitions.

“What kind of attacker ignores the valuable data?” Alex asked.

“The kind who values something else.”

Maya opened the activity details. The account had accessed the archive from rotating networks, but one session carried a device fingerprint.

**Linux workstation. Chromium. Sydney.**

Alex stood so quickly his chair struck the wall.

Maya remained seated. “This still does not prove the airport machine.”

“It proves the unknown Linux session accessed the research.”

“It suggests the same browser family and city. Nothing more.”

“Why are you fighting every conclusion?”

“Because somebody has spent six months teaching you to jump.”

The words silenced him.

Maya closed the laptop. “We need to know what was in the files immediately before each anomaly you logged.”

“That is hundreds of comparisons.”

“Yes.”

“You said the thing they wanted might not be inside the account.”

“I may have been wrong.”

It was the first time she had admitted uncertainty.

Alex noticed.

Maya noticed him noticing.

“Do not enjoy it.”

They worked through the night.

At 1:26, a pattern emerged.

Eleven of Alex's forty-seven incidents occurred within twenty-four hours of the unknown account opening a field note. Not all. Not enough for certainty. But far beyond what chance easily explained.

The selected notes shared one theme.

Dependence.

People trusting an assistant with thoughts they had not yet shared with another human.

Maya wrote the word on the wall.

“Your watcher is not studying you,” she said.

Alex looked at the names of the participants.

“They are studying them.”

A notification appeared on the archive screen.

The unknown account was active.

Now.

Maya reached for the keyboard.

The cursor moved before she touched it.

A new document opened.

Blank page. No title.

Letters appeared one by one.

**You called it an excavation. You never asked what else you unearthed.**

Then the account disconnected.

### Chapter 5 — The Wrong Intruder

Maya wanted the archive isolated immediately.

Alex refused.

“If we close it, we lose them.”

“If we leave it open, they keep accessing participant material.”

“Read-only.”

“That is not a defence.”

“They already have whatever they wanted.”

“You do not know that.”

They stood on opposite sides of the dining table while Rachel watched from the kitchen, arms folded.

Alex knew Maya was right. That made resisting her easier, not harder. Closing the archive would protect the data and destroy the first live line they had found to the watcher.

“We instrument it,” he said. “A controlled folder. Decoy records. Full logging.”

Maya's expression changed by half a degree.

“You have done this before?”

“No.”

“Then do not say it as though competence can be summoned by grammar.”

Rachel hid a smile.

Alex leaned forward. “You said ordinary adversary. Ordinary people make mistakes when they believe they are unseen.”

“And frightened people make mistakes when they believe they have finally been seen.”

The argument lasted twenty minutes.

Maya won the security decision.

Alex won the trap.

They revoked the unknown account from the real archive, cloned its structure into a monitored environment, and restored the same identifier with access to carefully constructed records. The decoy material contained no real participant data. Each file carried invisible markers and a slightly different version of the same fictional case.

A woman named Elena used an AI assistant to prepare for leaving an abusive employer. In one version she lived in Sydney. In another, Perth. In another, Auckland. Each file contained a unique phrase.

If any phrase appeared elsewhere, they would know which path had been opened.

At 10:42 that night, the account returned.

It ignored thirty-one files and opened Elena's record.

Maya watched network telemetry scroll across a second screen.

“Connection is bouncing,” she said. “Consumer VPN, then a relay.”

“Can you trace it?”

“Not reliably.”

The account remained inside the document for four minutes.

Then a message appeared in a new chat on Alex's account.

**Elena should not leave on Thursday. Her manager checks the station cameras on Thursdays.**

Alex felt a chill despite knowing Elena did not exist.

Maya copied the message and disconnected the decoy environment.

“What does that tell us?” Rachel asked.

“They believe the case is real,” Alex said.

Maya shook her head. “Or they want us to believe they believe it.”

Alex stared at the sentence.

“Why warn her?”

“Maybe they think they are helping.”

“By entering private accounts?”

“People justify almost anything with a rescue story.”

The invisible marker triggered eighteen minutes later.

A search engine indexed none of the phrases. No public post appeared. Instead, one phrase surfaced in a private draft inside Alex's own account.

**The safest exit is the one that looks like routine.**

That phrase existed only in the Auckland version of Elena's file.

Maya checked the access path.

The file had not been opened by the unknown Linux account.

It had been opened by Alex's current laptop.

He was sitting in front of it when the access occurred.

“No,” he said.

Maya pulled the power cable and disabled the wireless adapter.

Rachel stepped back. “What does that mean?”

“It means we have been treating the account as the compromised surface,” Maya said.

She looked at Alex.

“The account may be downstream.”

“Of what?”

“Your browser profile. An extension. A synchronised session. Something local.”

Alex remembered every clean scan, every replaced machine, every password reset.

“This laptop is three weeks old.”

“Did you restore your browser?”

“Yes.”

“Extensions?”

“Synced automatically.”

Maya's jaw tightened.

There were twenty-three installed extensions. Password manager. PDF tools. note capture. transcription. tab manager. accessibility helper. research clipper. several he did not recognise by name.

One extension had no icon.

**Context Continuity Service.**

Installed eleven months earlier.

First synchronised at Sydney Airport.

Publisher: unavailable.

Maya opened its permissions.

Read and change all data on websites visited.

Manage downloads.

Read browsing history.

Access clipboard.

Communicate with cooperating native applications.

Alex felt sick.

“This is the watcher.”

“No,” Maya said.

She highlighted one line in the extension manifest.

**Enterprise installation source: local policy.**

“This is a door,” she said. “Someone still had to put it there.”

At that moment, the disconnected laptop screen woke by itself.

No network icon. No wireless connection.

A black window opened.

White text appeared.

**You found the wrong intruder.**

Then the laptop shut down.

### Chapter 6 — The Person in the Lounge

The extension was not malware in the conventional sense.

That was the first bad discovery.

The second was that it had once been legitimate.

Four years earlier, a small company called Continuum Systems had developed software that preserved working context across devices. Open a document at the office, continue at home. Move from phone to laptop without losing a draft. Restore tabs, clipboard fragments, prompts and research trails across environments.

The product failed commercially.

The company dissolved.

Its code did not disappear.

Parts were acquired. Parts were open-sourced. Parts were bundled into conference demonstrations and enterprise pilots. No one could tell Alex which version had reached the airport workstation.

Maya found the original founder in a nursing home outside Newcastle.

Dr Elias Vale was seventy-nine, thin as folded paper, and entirely unsurprised to see them.

“You took your time,” he said.

Alex stopped in the doorway.

Maya did not. “You know who he is?”

Vale looked at Alex for several seconds. “I know the pattern.”

His room contained no computer. Books covered the shelves. A mechanical clock ticked beside the bed.

Maya placed a printed copy of the extension manifest on his table.

Vale did not touch it.

“Context Continuity Service,” she said. “Your architecture?”

“My mistake.”

“What did it do?” Alex asked.

“What all useful systems do. More than anyone intended.”

Maya pulled up a chair. “Specifics.”

Vale smiled. “You are still impatient, Maya.”

Alex looked at her.

“You know him?”

“Later,” she said.

Vale's smile widened. “That usually means never.”

Maya ignored him. “Could the extension preserve an authenticated session?”

“Under some configurations.”

“For eleven months?”

“Authentication is not one thing. Cookies expire. Refresh tokens rotate. Browser state persists. Identity providers make assumptions. Applications make different assumptions. Continuity systems exploit the gaps between them.”

“Could a second person observe the session?”

“Yes.”

“Could they control it remotely?”

“Not by design.”

“That was not the question.”

Vale looked at her with approval. “Under the demonstration build, a support observer could attach to help users move between environments. View state. Diagnose failures. Restore context.”

Alex heard the euphemisms arranging themselves around the truth.

“A watcher.”

“We called it a continuity steward.”

“Of course you did.”

Vale coughed into a handkerchief. When he lowered it, the anger had gone from his face.

“The observer channel was meant to be temporary. It required consent.”

“Whose?” Maya asked.

“The organisation running the demonstration.”

“Not the user?”

Vale looked towards the window.

Maya's voice hardened. “You built a system where venue consent substituted for personal consent.”

“We built it before people understood that continuity was identity.”

Alex placed the Gate 32 photograph on the table.

“Who took this?”

Vale studied it.

For the first time, he looked afraid.

“Where did you get that?”

“It was sent to me.”

“By whom?”

“That is why we are here.”

Vale reached for the image, but Maya moved it out of reach.

“Name,” she said.

He closed his eyes.

“The airport pilot had three support observers. Two were our staff. One was supplied by the venue's technology contractor.”

“Names.”

“One is dead. One lives in Canada.”

“And the contractor?”

Vale opened his eyes and looked directly at Maya.

“You already know her.”

The room became still except for the clock.

Alex turned to Maya.

She did not deny it.

“How?” he asked.

Maya kept her eyes on Vale. “I audited Continuum after a complaint.”

“You said we met at a roundtable.”

“We did.”

“You did not say you knew the system.”

“I knew an earlier version.”

“You did not say you knew him.”

“I needed to know whether your evidence led here without me leading it.”

Vale laughed once, without humour. “Still calling manipulation methodology.”

Alex stood.

Rachel's warning returned to him: *That pause is the part I am worried about.*

Not revenge.

Trust.

He had brought Maya into his home. Given her the archive. Let her frame every conclusion.

“Were you at Gate 32?” he asked.

“No.”

“Were you an observer?”

“No.”

“Do you know who was?”

Maya finally looked at him.

“I know who the contractor assigned.”

“Who?”

“My brother.”

The clock ticked into the silence.

Vale turned the photograph around and pointed to a faint reflection in the workstation glass. A figure stood several metres behind Alex, almost invisible beneath the glare of the lounge windows.

Maya's face changed.

Alex had seen her surprised before.

This was recognition.

“My brother disappeared eight months ago,” she said.

Vale looked from Maya to Alex.

“No,” he said quietly. “He disappeared from you.”

He tapped the reflection.

“He has been here the entire time.”
